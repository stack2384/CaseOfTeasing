# Lean Leading, or Leadership? Why Light Authority Lasts

Rasha

Winter 2026 · Prepared for an undergraduate lecture

## Abstract

Leadership is one of the most discussed ideas in organizational studies, yet it is used to mean very different things. This paper separates *leadership*, a noun that names a position or quality, from *leading*, a verb that names an act. It proposes the idea of **lean leading**: exercising authority with as little weight as the task allows, so that information flows freely, decisions can be explained, power can be handed on, and members can leave or object without penalty. The argument draws on classic work in sociology, management and psychology, and on a short look at history from the ancient Near East to the present. It also tests the idea against its strongest objections. The paper ends with a classroom case and discussion questions.

**Keywords:** leadership, leading, governance, lean thinking, authority, information flow, ethics

## Learning Objectives

After reading this paper, a student should be able to:

1. Distinguish leadership as position, as influence and as service.
2. Explain what “lean” means when applied to leading rather than to manufacturing.
3. Compare governance with an enforcement hierarchy by following how information flows.
4. Name at least four judgement biases that distort how we assess leaders.
5. Argue for and against lean leading using evidence.

## 1. Introduction

Bookshops, universities and corporate workshops all sell leadership. Yet when people describe their own workplaces, clubs or teams, they often say they feel managed, informed late, or judged by people they have never met. The gap between how much leadership is taught and how rarely it is felt is the starting point of this paper. This is an observation rather than a measured statistic, and a student could test it with a survey.

Governance keeps an organization orderly and lawful. It does not by itself make anyone want to follow. Weber (1922/1978) showed that authority can rest on tradition, charisma or rules, and any of these can operate while people feel unled. The question asked here is what kind of authority is light enough to last.

## 2. Key Terms

- **Leadership:** a noun. A position, rank or quality that someone holds.
- **Leading:** a verb. The act of going first, carrying weight and making the path walkable for others.
- **Governance:** the rules, offices and procedures by which an organization is directed and held accountable.
- **Enforcement hierarchy:** a structure in which instructions flow down and compliance flows up, and in which bad news is softened at each level.
- **Lean leading:** the practice of using the least authority the task needs, and making that authority visible, explainable and transferable.

The words themselves carry history. *Lead* comes from an Old English verb meaning to go before or bring along, while *leadership* is a later noun that became common in the nineteenth century (Oxford English Dictionary, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). *Govern* traces back through Latin to a Greek word for steering a ship, and shares that root with *cybernetics*, the study of steering through feedback (Wiener, 1948/1961). Origins do not fix present meaning, but they reveal what a word first assumed.

## 3. Three Definitions of Leadership

Most arguments about leaders are really arguments between three definitions.

**Leadership as position.** You lead because you hold the chair. Weber’s (1922/1978) legal-rational authority is the clearest case: the office carries the power.

**Leadership as influence.** You lead because others move when you move. Northouse (2021) surveys many definitions of this kind, which centre on a person influencing a group toward a goal. Carlyle (1841) pushed this view to its extreme by arguing that history is shaped by exceptional individuals.

**Leadership as service.** You lead because others can do more because you are there. Greenleaf (1970) argued that the leader should be a servant first, and that the test is whether those served grow.

A useful habit is to ask, in any dispute, which definition each side is using. A board may expect the first, a team may long for the third, and a public relations office may advertise the second.

### 3.1 Opinion, bylaws and election

Three instruments usually stand in for a definition. *Opinion* is how people feel about someone: fast, informal and easy to manipulate. *Bylaws* record who may do what, and also record past fears. *Election* confers legitimacy but does not guarantee good use of power. Opinion without bylaws is mood, bylaws without opinion is paperwork, and election without either is theatre.

Michels (1911/1915) argued that even democratic organizations tend to concentrate control in a few hands over time. This claim is influential and also contested, which makes it a good point for debate.

## 4. What “Lean” Adds

The word *lean* comes into management from manufacturing. Ohno (1988) described the Toyota production system as an effort to remove waste, and Womack and Jones (1996) distilled lean thinking into five ideas: define value from the customer’s point of view, map the stream of activities that produce it, make work flow, let demand pull the work, and keep improving. Liker (2004) added that the Toyota approach rests on continuous improvement together with respect for people.

This paper borrows the spirit of lean, not its factory methods. Applied to leading, the idea becomes four principles.

1. **Value:** define what the group exists to deliver, and measure authority by whether it helps deliver it.
2. **Waste:** remove layers, titles, meetings and secrets that add weight without adding value.
3. **Flow:** let information move both ways, so that problems reach someone who can act.
4. **Respect and renewal:** explain decisions, share the work, train successors and plan exits.

**Caution.** Lean can be misused as a slogan for cutting staff. The sense used here is the opposite: it asks the leader to carry less, not the members to carry more.

## 5. A Brief Historical View

History does not offer a ladder of ever better leadership. It offers a rotation of models: authority based on skill, on lineage and sacred sanction, on assemblies and councils, on bureaucracy, and now on networks. A recurring pattern is that concentrated authority is efficient and fragile, while distributed authority is slower and more resilient.

Some selected points, with approximate dates:

- **c. 2126 BC:** Gudea governs the city-state of Lagash and records his temple building in long inscriptions (Edzard, 1997).
- **c. 2112 BC onward:** Ur-Nammu founds the Third Dynasty of Ur, and an early law collection is attributed to him (Roth, 1997).
- **508–507 BC:** Reforms of Cleisthenes in Athens spread authority across citizens (Ober, 2015).
- **1215 AD:** Magna Carta binds a ruler to law (Holt, 1992).
- **1748 AD:** Montesquieu argues for separating powers (Montesquieu, 1748/1989).
- **1911 AD:** Taylor’s scientific management treats work as something to be designed from above (Taylor, 1911).
- **1948 AD:** Wiener’s *Cybernetics* recasts governance as feedback and steering (Wiener, 1948/1961).
- **1970 AD:** Greenleaf’s essay frames leading as service (Greenleaf, 1970).
- **1999 AD:** Edmondson’s research on psychological safety measures how safe it is to speak up (Edmondson, 1999).

### 5.1 Thinking from 2126 BC

To see how much of our thinking is inherited, imagine standing in Lagash around 2126 BC. A person there would not call the year “2126 BC.” Mesopotamian scribes named years after events, and Egyptians counted by the reign of the king (Hornung et al., 2006). Our own numbering, the *Anno Domini* count, was proposed by the monk Dionysius Exiguus in the sixth century AD and has no year zero, so the span from 2126 BC to 2026 AD is about 4,151 years (Blackburn & Holford-Strevens, 1999).

The observer in Lagash had already seen large central states fall, including the Akkadian empire and the Egyptian Old Kingdom (Roux, 1992). What persisted were temples, granaries, scribal habits and local assemblies. The lesson visible from that position is that offices come and go while working routines survive. Dates before about 1000 BC vary by chronology, and the surviving texts were written by elites, so a record there is not the same as what happened.

## 6. How Information Flows: Governance or Enforcement

The simplest way to tell governance from enforcement is to follow information.

In **governance**, information moves both ways. Those at the top learn what is happening below, those below learn why decisions were made, and correction is safe.

In an **enforcement hierarchy**, orders move down and compliance moves up. Bad news is softened at each level, so a leader may feel informed while knowing very little.

Three diagnostic questions help:

1. How long does a warning take to reach someone who can act?
2. What happens to the person who delivers it?
3. Can anyone below the top explain the reason for the last major decision?

Edmondson (1999) found that psychological safety was associated with learning behavior in work teams, although measures and replications are still debated. Wiener (1948/1961) argued more generally that a system steered without accurate feedback drifts.

## 7. Deciding, Judging and the Table Bell

Deciding commits resources to the future. Judging evaluates conduct in the past. When one small group does both, it writes the rule, applies it and grades itself. Montesquieu (1748/1989) argued for separating such powers, and members of any organization should be able to ask four questions. Who sits on the deciding committee? By what criteria were they chosen? Who reviews them? What can a member do if a decision seems unfair?

A related problem can be called **the table bell**. Many significant decisions are formed before the public meeting, in corridors, calls and small gatherings. Then a formal meeting is held and ratifies what was effectively settled. This is not always sinister, because preparation saves time. It becomes a problem when the pre-meeting is the real meeting. The claim here rests on observation more than on a single study, though it is consistent with Michels’ (1911/1915) argument about concentrated control. Students should test it against cases they know, asking in each whether the private conversation was *preparation* or *replacement*.

## 8. Ethics, Morality and Going with the Flow

Ethics (what a group agrees to be accountable for) and morality (what a person privately holds right) are often used as if they were the same. Both words come from words for custom: Greek *ethos* and Latin *mores* (Oxford English Dictionary, n.d.-c). A third path is simply to go with the flow, doing what the room is doing.

Janis (1972) analyzed how cohesive groups reached poor decisions through groupthink, and Asch (1951) showed how group pressure can change what individuals say they see. Milgram (1963) showed how readily people could defer to authority in an experimental setting, although the interpretation and ethics of those studies are debated. The practical lesson is that most failures are not caused by villains. They are caused by decent people in structures where dissent is costly. A leader’s ethical work is therefore to make dissent affordable, and a good test is to ask when someone last said no and what happened to them afterwards.

Hirschman (1970) added that members facing decline can leave, speak up or stay silent. Where exit is costly, voice becomes dangerous and silence becomes common. This is why lean leading includes an **escape route**: a legitimate way to appeal, to leave without retaliation, and to remove a leader without a crisis.

## 9. Judgement Bias: Watching How We Watch

Our judgements about leaders are shaped by habits of thought. Five are especially useful for students.

- **Hindsight bias:** outcomes make past decisions look obviously good or obviously bad (Fischhoff, 1975).
- **Confirmation bias:** we notice evidence that fits our view and overlook the rest (Nickerson, 1998).
- **Halo effect:** people we like receive softer judgment (Thorndike, 1920).
- **Self-serving bias:** we tend to see our own agenda as aligned with the group’s (Miller & Ross, 1975).
- **Presentism and Whig history:** judging the past by today’s standards, or reading history as a steady march toward the present (Butterfield, 1931).

Another distortion is survivorship: we study institutions that lasted or left records and miss those that quietly failed. Every observer also has an angle. A leader, a middle manager, a newcomer at the door and a historian looking back each see different things, and good analysis names its angle.

## 10. Objections and Limits

A strong paper tests its own thesis. Four objections deserve a reply.

**Objection 1: Crises need decisive, even heavy authority.** This is often true. Lean leading does not rule out firm decisions. It asks that the authority be explained, limited in time, and returned when the crisis ends.

**Objection 2: Leadership means more than staying out of the way.** Heifetz (1994) argued that leaders must sometimes push a group to face hard problems it would prefer to avoid. Kotter (1990) distinguished leadership, which copes with change, from management, which copes with complexity. Lean leading must therefore not become passivity. “Lean” describes the weight of the authority, not the amount of effort.

**Objection 3: Shared leadership has costs.** Pearce and Conger (2003) and Raelin (2003) describe shared or collective leadership, but sharing can slow decisions and blur accountability. The reply is that structure matters: shared work still needs clear owners.

**Objection 4: The evidence is selective.** The historical examples above lean toward Mesopotamia, Egypt, China and the Mediterranean, and many traditions of rotating and shared authority are under-represented. Several claims, especially about hidden decision making, are arguments to test rather than facts to repeat.

## 11. Classroom Case and Discussion

**Case: The Student Society Committee.** A university society has a president, a treasurer and a secretary. For two years, all important decisions have been made in a small group chat among the three officers. The monthly general meeting votes on whatever is presented, and almost always agrees. A first-year member raises a concern about how funds were used, and is told that the matter is “already handled.” She stops attending.

**Questions for discussion**

1. Which definition of leadership (position, influence or service) is the committee using? Which would the first-year member prefer?
2. Trace how information moved in this case. Is it governance or an enforcement hierarchy, and what is the evidence?
3. Where is the table bell, and where is the doorbell?
4. Which judgement biases might affect how the officers view themselves, and how the member views them?
5. Using the four lean principles (value, waste, flow, respect and renewal), propose three changes. What might go wrong with each?
6. What escape route would you build for the member and for the president?

**Suggested 50-minute plan:** 10 minutes on definitions and key terms; 10 minutes on lean principles and information flow; 5 minutes on history and the 2126 BC exercise; 15 minutes on small-group work on the case; 10 minutes on objections and wrap-up.

## 12. Conclusion

Leadership as a standing noun encourages titles and ceremony. Leading as a verb means going first, making the path visible, sharing the reasons for decisions and leaving a structure that works without you. Lean leading is a practical form of this: use the least authority the task needs, let information travel both ways, separate deciding from judging, make the real decision path visible, and plan exits for everyone, including yourself.

The idea is not beyond criticism, and the objections above should be taken seriously. What the long view of history suggests is modest but useful: what persists is usually what was light enough to carry on.
